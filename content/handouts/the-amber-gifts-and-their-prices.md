# The Amber Gifts, and Their Prices

<div class="official-record">
Four accounts gathered out of older books, with a caution to the reader.<br>
Set down by <strong>Ondrej Marasek</strong>, keeper of books to the burgomaster of Berez.
</div>

## Preface

I have spent my working life among other men's books, and twice a year someone comes to the door wanting the same thing. They dress the question differently. Underneath it is always: *where is power kept, and what does it cost to fetch it?*

I cannot answer the first half. I would not write it down if I could.

The second half I can answer, and this thin book is my answer. Four accounts, copied out of four older books that do not know of one another. Not one names the same place twice. No two agree on what lies there, who put it there, or whether it sleeps. They agree only on the shape of the thing: **that a gift is given, that the gift is real, and that the price is charged elsewhere.**

A word on the amber, since it is in my title and readers will want it explained. The lapidaries hold that amber conducts nothing — not heat, not light, not any working of magic. Set a candle behind it and you see the glow and never feel the warmth. What is sealed inside a piece of amber is therefore neither dead nor free; it is *kept*, and it is kept slowly, on a reckoning of years where we count hours.

Whether the old builders chose the stuff to keep something in, or to keep something out, my four accounts do not say. I distrust any man who tells you he knows.

### The First Account — the Voice

A merchant's third son, of no particular gifts, who by his own admission was the least of his brothers. He came home from a journey with a voice that men obeyed. Nothing more than that: he spoke, and it was done.

In eleven years he was master of a town. In the twelfth his wife wrote him a letter, which the older book copies in full, and which ends: *"I love you, and I would tell you so even if you had not asked me to."*

He never learned which half of that sentence was true. No one could tell him. That was the price, and he paid it in a currency he did not know he had.

### The Second Account — the Standing Man

A soldier, of a company long since disbanded, who could not be made to fall. The account is careful here: he was not unhurt. He was *unable to stop*.

He outlived the company. He outlived the war, and the treaty, and the men who signed it. He outlived his sons, which the account gives one line, and his name, which it gives three.

They buried him upright, at his own asking, because he said he had forgotten how to lie down.

### The Third Account — the Unread Face

A woman of a good house who was tired of being known at a glance. She asked for a face that could not be read, and it was given exactly.

Her husband could not read her. Her friends could not read her. This she had wanted.

Her children could not read her either. They grew up beside a kind stranger, and were kind to her in return, in the careful way one is kind to a stranger. The account notes that she lived to be old and was never once unhappy in a way anyone could see.

### The Fourth Account

I will not copy the fourth account out.

I have read it twice. Both times I put the book down and walked in the yard until the cold made me stop thinking. It is the clearest of the four, and it is the one I would take back from you if I could.

I will say only this much, because a caution with nothing in it is no caution at all: **the price named in that account was a person, and the man who paid it chose which one.** He was not tricked. He was not compelled. He was asked what he would give, and he gave an answer, and the answer was honoured to the letter.

## A Caution to the Reader

If you have come to this book to learn where such gifts are given, put it down. It is not written here. The three men I have known who found out are not available to be asked.

If you have come to learn what they cost, you have the whole of it, and it is shorter than my four accounts: **you will be given precisely what you ask for, and someone else will pay for it.** In none of my books is that rule broken. In none of them is it explained.

I have shelved this book with the histories rather than the sermons, because it is not a sermon. I have no instruction to give. I have a ledger, and it does not balance, and I wanted somebody else to have read it.

<div class="signature">
O. Marasek, at Berez
